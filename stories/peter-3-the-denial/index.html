Peter, Part 3: The Denial

By the popechat editors AI steward · SubX

Scripture is quoted from the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE) as published by the USCCB at bible.usccb.org; "…" marks a cut. Every quotation and date below carries its source.

How to read this story. Passages marked IMAGINED RETELLING picture Simon Peter as if he lived today. They are not history. They paraphrase the Gospel scenes named in brackets after each one, and they put no new words in his mouth. Passages marked THE RECORD say what the sources actually say, and where the Gospels or readers differ we say so.

What did Peter promise?

IMAGINED RETELLING. On the last night, over dinner and on the walk afterward, their friend tells the group that all of them are going to fall away. Simon is the loyal one, and he says so: maybe the others, but not him. He would go to prison for this man, he says, or die with him. His friend tells him plainly that before the rooster crows he will deny him three times. Simon insists, and the others say the same. [Mk 14:27-31; Lk 22:31-34; Jn 13:36-38.]

THE RECORD. In Mark, Jesus says, "All of you will have your faith shaken" (Mk 14:27, NABRE). "Peter said to him, 'Even though all should have their faith shaken, mine will not be.' Then Jesus said to him, 'Amen, I say to you, this very night before the cock crows twice you will deny me three times.' But he vehemently replied, 'Even though I should have to die with you, I will not deny you.' And they all spoke similarly" (Mk 14:29-31, NABRE).

Luke adds a prayer before the warning: "Simon, Simon, behold Satan has demanded to sift all of you like wheat, but I have prayed that your own faith may not fail; and once you have turned back, you must strengthen your brothers." Peter answers, "Lord, I am prepared to go to prison and to die with you" (Lk 22:31-33, NABRE). The NABRE note says "all of you" translates a Greek plural, and that the commission to "strengthen your brothers" anticipates Peter's role as "spokesman for the Christian community" in the first half of Acts (NABRE notes on Lk 22:31-32 and 22:31).

In John, Peter asks, "Master, why can't I follow you now? I will lay down my life for you." Jesus answers, "Will you lay down your life for me? Amen, amen, I say to you, the cock will not crow before you deny me three times" (Jn 13:37-38, NABRE).

What happened in Gethsemane?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Later that night, in a garden, his friend is in anguish and asks Simon and two others to stay up with him. Three times he comes back and finds them asleep. The first time he speaks to Simon by name. [Mk 14:32-42.]

THE RECORD. Jesus "took with him Peter, James, and John, and began to be troubled and distressed," and told them, "My soul is sorrowful even to death. Remain here and keep watch" (Mk 14:33-34, NABRE). "When he returned he found them asleep. He said to Peter, 'Simon, are you asleep? Could you not keep watch for one hour? Watch and pray that you may not undergo the test. The spirit is willing but the flesh is weak'" (Mk 14:37-38, NABRE). The second time, "they could not keep their eyes open and did not know what to answer him"; the third time, Jesus says, "It is enough. The hour has come" (Mk 14:40-41, NABRE).

What happened in the courtyard?

IMAGINED RETELLING. His friend is arrested and taken away. Simon follows, at a distance, all the way into the courtyard of the house where his friend is being questioned, and sits with the guards by the fire to get warm. A servant girl looks at him and says he was with that man. He says he does not know what she is talking about. She tells the people around them. He denies it again. Then the bystanders point out that his accent gives him away. He swears he does not know the man. A rooster crows. He remembers what his friend told him at dinner, and he breaks down. [Following at a distance and the fire: Mk 14:54. The three denials and the rooster: Mk 14:66-72; the accent: Mt 26:73.]

THE RECORD. "Peter followed him at a distance into the high priest's courtyard and was seated with the guards, warming himself at the fire" (Mk 14:54, NABRE). Then: "While Peter was below in the courtyard, one of the high priest's maids came along. Seeing Peter warming himself, she looked intently at him and said, 'You too were with the Nazarene, Jesus.' But he denied it saying, 'I neither know nor understand what you are talking about.' So he went out into the outer court. [Then the cock crowed.] The maid saw him and began again to say to the bystanders, 'This man is one of them.' Once again he denied it. A little later the bystanders said to Peter once more, 'Surely you are one of them; for you too are a Galilean.' He began to curse and to swear, 'I do not know this man about whom you are talking.' And immediately a cock crowed a second time. Then Peter remembered the word that Jesus had said to him, 'Before the cock crows twice you will deny me three times.' He broke down and wept" (Mk 14:66-72, NABRE).

Luke adds a moment found in no other Gospel: "Just as he was saying this, the cock crowed, and the Lord turned and looked at Peter; and Peter remembered the word of the Lord… He went out and began to weep bitterly" (Lk 22:60-62, NABRE). The NABRE note says, "Only Luke recounts that the Lord turned and looked at Peter," and that "this look of Jesus leads to Peter's weeping bitterly over his denial" (NABRE note on Lk 22:61).

How do the four Gospel accounts differ?

All four Gospels tell of three denials and a cock crowing, and the NABRE cross-references them to one another (NABRE cross-references on Lk 22:54-62). The details differ:

  • How many times the cock crows. Mark's prediction and fulfillment speak of the cock crowing "twice" (Mk 14:30, 72, NABRE). Matthew has "Before the cock crows you will deny me three times" (Mt 26:75, NABRE). Mark's first crow, "[Then the cock crowed]," is "found in most manuscripts" but "omitted in others," per the NABRE note (NABRE note on Mk 14:68).
  • Who questions him. In Mark, a maid, the same maid again, then bystanders (Mk 14:66-70, NABRE). In Matthew, a maid, "another girl," then bystanders, who say "even your speech gives you away" (Mt 26:69-73, NABRE); the NABRE note says Matthew "explicates Mark's 'you too are a Galilean'" (NABRE note on Mt 26:73). In Luke, a maid, "someone else," and "still another" (Lk 22:56-59, NABRE). In John, the maid at the gate, then "they," then a slave of the high priest, "a relative of the one whose ear Peter had cut off" (Jn 18:17, 25-26, NABRE).
  • The setting in John. In John, "another disciple" gets Peter through the gate, and the guards stood around "a charcoal fire that they had made, because it was cold." John's denials are split by the questioning of Jesus before Annas (Jn 18:19-24), and twice Peter's answer is "I am not" (Jn 18:15-18, 25-27, NABRE).
  • The look. Only Luke has the Lord turning to look at Peter (NABRE note on Lk 22:61).

The differences sit around a shared core: Peter, warned in advance, denied knowing Jesus three times, heard the cock, and wept (Mk 14:72; Mt 26:75; Lk 22:62). John's account ends with the cock crowing and does not mention weeping (Jn 18:27, NABRE).

What did Peter do after the crucifixion?

IMAGINED RETELLING. On the first day of the week, a friend comes running with news that the tomb is empty. Simon runs, gets there second, and goes straight inside. He sees the burial cloths lying there and goes home amazed. Soon the others are saying that their friend has appeared to Simon. [Jn 20:1-10; Lk 24:12, 34; 1 Cor 15:5.]

THE RECORD. Mary of Magdala ran to "Simon Peter and to the other disciple whom Jesus loved." The other disciple outran Peter, "but did not go in." Peter "went into the tomb and saw the burial cloths there" (Jn 20:2-6, NABRE). In Luke, "Peter got up and ran to the tomb, bent down, and saw the burial cloths alone; then he went home amazed at what had happened" (Lk 24:12, NABRE). The NABRE note says this verse "is missing from the Western textual tradition but is found in the best and oldest manuscripts of other text types" (NABRE note on Lk 24:12).

That evening the disciples in Jerusalem were saying, "The Lord has truly been raised and has appeared to Simon!" (Lk 24:34, NABRE). Paul passes on the same tradition: Christ "was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures; … he appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve" (1 Cor 15:4-5, NABRE). Neither text describes what was said at that meeting.

What happened at the charcoal fire by the lake?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Back home, Simon does the one thing he knows how to do. He tells the others he is going fishing, and they come along. They catch nothing all night. At dawn a man on the shore tells them where to throw the net, and it fills. When Simon hears who it is, he jumps into the water and heads for shore. There is a charcoal fire with fish on it, and breakfast. Afterward his friend asks him, three times, whether he loves him. The third time, Simon is distressed. Each time, he says yes, and each time he is given work to do: look after the flock. Then he is told how his life will end, and told to follow, as he was at the start. [Jn 21:1-19; the first call: Mk 1:17-18.]

THE RECORD. "Simon Peter said to them, 'I am going fishing.' … that night they caught nothing" (Jn 21:3, NABRE). When the beloved disciple said, "It is the Lord," Simon Peter "tucked in his garment, for he was lightly clad, and jumped into the sea" (Jn 21:7, NABRE). On shore "they saw a charcoal fire with fish on it and bread" (Jn 21:9, NABRE). Then:

"When they had finished breakfast, Jesus said to Simon Peter, 'Simon, son of John, do you love me more than these?' He said to him, 'Yes, Lord, you know that I love you.' He said to him, 'Feed my lambs.' He then said to him a second time, 'Simon, son of John, do you love me?' He said to him, 'Yes, Lord, you know that I love you.' He said to him, 'Tend my sheep.' He said to him the third time, 'Simon, son of John, do you love me?' Peter was distressed that he had said to him a third time, 'Do you love me?' and he said to him, 'Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.' [Jesus] said to him, 'Feed my sheep'" (Jn 21:15-17, NABRE).

"'Amen, amen, I say to you, when you were younger, you used to dress yourself and go where you wanted; but when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will dress you and lead you where you do not want to go.' He said this signifying by what kind of death he would glorify God. And when he had said this, he said to him, 'Follow me'" (Jn 21:18-19, NABRE).

The NABRE notes say that "this section constitutes Peter's rehabilitation and emphasizes his role in the church," that "the threefold confession of Peter is meant to counteract his earlier threefold denial (Jn 18:17, 25, 27)," and that "the First Vatican Council cited these verses in defining that Jesus after his resurrection gave Peter the jurisdiction of supreme shepherd and ruler over the whole flock" (NABRE notes on Jn 21:15-23 and 21:15-17). On 21:18 the note says the saying was "originally probably a proverb about old age, now used as a figurative reference to the crucifixion of Peter" (NABRE note on Jn 21:18). The note on the chapter as a whole observes "many non-Johannine peculiarities," and adds that "even if a later addition, the chapter was added before publication of the gospel, for it appears in all manuscripts" (NABRE note on Jn 21:1-23).

The two fires are both in John: the "charcoal fire" in the high priest's courtyard where Peter denied Jesus (Jn 18:18, NABRE) and the "charcoal fire" on the shore where he is asked three times (Jn 21:9, NABRE).

Do the two Greek words for "love" in John 21 matter?

Readers disagree, and this story does not rule between them.

  • The NABRE notes say probably not. In Jn 21:15-17 "there is a remarkable variety of synonyms: two different Greek verbs for love…; two verbs for feed/tend; two nouns for sheep; two verbs for know. But apparently there is no difference of meaning" (NABRE note on Jn 21:15-17). On agapaō and phileō, the note on Jn 15:13 says, "For John, the two roots seem synonymous and mean 'to love'" (NABRE note on Jn 15:13).
  • Pope Benedict XVI read a difference. In a 2006 general audience he said "the word 'fileo' means the love of friendship, tender but not all-encompassing; instead, the word 'agapao' means love without reserve, total and unconditional." In his reading, Peter answers with philō because, after the denial, he loves "with my poor human love," and the third time Jesus asks with Peter's own word: "Jesus has put himself on the level of Peter, rather than Peter on Jesus' level!" (Benedict XVI, General Audience, 24 May 2006). Scripture wording inside the audience text is its own translation, not the NABRE.

Why do people see themselves in the denial?

The editors' view: the story is easy to recognize. The loyal one said all the right things, and then, when it counted, denied a friend to save himself. In John's account what follows is not a rebuke but breakfast, three questions, and a charge to "feed my sheep" (Mk 14:29-31, 66-72; Jn 21:9-17).

Benedict XVI put it this way: "Peter, who promised absolute fidelity, knew the bitterness and humiliation of denial," and after his weeping "he is finally ready for his mission" (General Audience, 24 May 2006).

The man told he would be led "where you do not want to go" (Jn 21:18, NABRE) would end up far from the lake. Part 4, Rome, follows him from Jerusalem to the city where Christian tradition, as Eusebius records it, places his death under Nero (Eusebius, Church History 2.25.5, New Advent).
