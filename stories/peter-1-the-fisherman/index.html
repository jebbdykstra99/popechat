Peter, Part 1: The Fisherman

By the popechat editors

popechat.com · Story 01a · Urbi et orbi.

popechat is an independent Catholic community room. It is not an official Vatican outlet, it does not speak for the Church, and it does not replace Mass. Scripture is quoted from the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE) as published by the USCCB at bible.usccb.org; "…" marks a cut. Every quotation and date below carries its source.

How to read this story. Passages marked IMAGINED RETELLING picture Simon Peter as if he were working the lake today. They are not history. They paraphrase the Gospel scenes named in brackets after each one, and they put no new words in his mouth. Passages marked THE RECORD say what the sources actually say, and where scholars disagree we say so.

Why does this room start with Peter?

Because every story in this series stands on his. This room chose to begin its ten-pope series with the man the Church counts first: the Holy See lists Peter as "1st Pope of the Catholic Church" (Vatican.va). That is popechat's choice of where to begin, not an endorsement from anyone in Rome.

In Matthew's Gospel, after Simon names Jesus as the Messiah, Jesus answers him: "And so I say to you, you are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church, and the gates of the netherworld shall not prevail against it" (Mt 16:18, NABRE). The rock is where we start. Peter gets four stories, because one is not enough: the fisherman, the rock, the denial, and Rome. This is the first.

Who was Simon before he was Peter?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Picture a guy with a fishing boat, a brother in the business, a mortgage on a house he shares with family, and a phone full of contacts at the dock. People know him by his first name and his dad's name. Nobody has ever called him anything grand. [Name and family: Mt 16:17; Jn 1:42; Jn 21:15. Brother in the trade: Mk 1:16. House and in-laws: Mk 1:29-30. The "phone" and "mortgage" are modern stand-ins, not history.]

THE RECORD. His name was Simon. Jesus addresses him as "Simon son of Jonah" (Mt 16:17, NABRE) and, in John's Gospel, "Simon the son of John" (Jn 1:42, NABRE) and "Simon, son of John" (Jn 21:15, NABRE). The new name came from Jesus: "you will be called Cephas" (which is translated Peter) (Jn 1:42, NABRE). Part 2 takes up that name.

When was he born? No one knows. The New Testament gives no birth date or year, and no season. The Holy See's own page on Peter lists his birthplace but no birth date (Vatican.va). The Encyclopedia of World Biography reasons that he was "a grown man" during Jesus' public life, which "would place his birth sometime around the end of the 1st century B.C." (Encyclopedia.com). Treat that as an estimate, not a date.

The days on the calendar are feasts, not birthdays. June 29 is the Solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul, Apostles (USCCB readings, 29 June 2026), and February 22 is the Feast of the Chair of Saint Peter, Apostle (USCCB readings, 22 Feb 2025).

Where did Peter grow up?

IMAGINED RETELLING. He comes from a lakeside fishing town that is being turned into a city, the kind of place where the new money moves in and the fishing families stay. Ask two archaeologists today which mound it was and you will get two answers. [Bethsaida: Jn 1:44. The town's upgrade to a city under Herod Philip, around 30 C.E.: Josephus, as summarized by the Biblical Archaeology Society, below.]

THE RECORD. John's Gospel says: "Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the town of Andrew and Peter" (Jn 1:44, NABRE). The Holy See and Vatican News give Bethsaida as his birthplace (Vatican.va; Vatican News, Saint of the Day, 29 June).

The Biblical Archaeology Society says that "around 30 C.E." Herod Philip "transformed this fishing village into a city," and that Josephus reports he renamed it Julias and increased its population and "grandeur" (Jewish Antiquities 18.28) (Biblical Archaeology Society).

Where Bethsaida stood is disputed. The Biblical Archaeology Society reports that "we don't know its exact location" and that "two archaeological sites—et-Tell and el-Araj—are contenders," both "excavated by reputable teams" whose leaders are each confident they have the real Bethsaida-Julias (Biblical Archaeology Society). Rami Arav leads at et-Tell; Mordechai Aviam and R. Steven Notley lead at el-Araj, where a Byzantine basilica was found (BAS; Notley, Novum Testamentum 64.4, 2022). Notley's team argues for el-Araj; the question is open.

What was Peter's business?

IMAGINED RETELLING. By the time we meet him, he has moved down the shore to another town on the lake. He runs a boat with his brother. Two other brothers, whose father has hired hands of his own, are his partners. It is a small business with thin margins: some nights you work till dawn and bring in nothing. [Capernaum and the house: Mk 1:21, 29. Brother Andrew, casting nets: Mk 1:16. Partners James and John: Lk 5:10. Zebedee's hired men: Mk 1:20. The empty night: Lk 5:5.]

THE RECORD. Mark's Gospel brings Jesus to Capernaum (Mk 1:21, NABRE) and then into "the house of Simon and Andrew with James and John" (Mk 1:29, NABRE). Mark first meets the brothers at work: "he saw Simon and his brother Andrew casting their nets into the sea; they were fishermen" (Mk 1:16, NABRE). Luke names the partnership: "James and John, the sons of Zebedee, who were partners of Simon" (Lk 5:10, NABRE), and Mark mentions Zebedee's "hired men" (Mk 1:20, NABRE).

The house at Capernaum (tradition and archaeology). Franciscan excavators led by Fr. Virgilio Corbo reopened the site in 1968 and found, beneath a Byzantine octagonal church, a house that had become a place of Christian gathering. The Custody of the Holy Land, which keeps the site, says that "the identification of the house of St. Peter is based on the combination of archaeological data and literary sources" (Custodia Terrae Sanctae). Not every scholar accepts every step: Notley, from the el-Araj side, argues that the Byzantine tradition placed Peter's home in Bethsaida, and he calls the octagonal-church identification an "innovation of Orfali in 1921" (Notley, 2022, abstract). So the house is possibly his: a tradition with archaeological support, not a certainty.

Was Peter married?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Yes, and his wife's mother lives with them. When a friend comes home with him after synagogue, she is sick in bed with a fever. [Mk 1:29-31.]

THE RECORD. "Simon's mother-in-law lay sick with a fever" (Mk 1:30, NABRE). Paul, years later, writes: "Do we not have the right to take along a Christian wife, as do the rest of the apostles, and the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas?" (1 Cor 9:5, NABRE).

Was Peter educated?

IMAGINED RETELLING. No degree, no title, and no standing in front of the people who run things. Later, when he speaks up anyway, the authorities notice exactly that. [Acts 4:13.]

THE RECORD. When Peter and John stand before the council in Jerusalem, Acts says the members were "observing the boldness of Peter and John and perceiving them to be uneducated, ordinary men" and "they were amazed" (Acts 4:13, NABRE). That is the council's impression as Acts reports it. It does not settle how much schooling Peter had or whether he could read and write, and the sources cited here do not say.

How did Jesus call Simon the fisherman?

IMAGINED RETELLING. It is the morning after a bad night. The nets are being washed. A teacher already drawing crowds steps into Simon's boat and asks him to push off a little so he can speak from the water. Afterward he tells Simon to go out deep and try again. Simon says they worked all night and caught nothing, and does it anyway. The haul nearly sinks two boats. Simon falls at the teacher's knees and tells him to leave, because he knows what kind of man he is. The answer: do not be afraid. The partners haul the boats in and walk away from all of it. [Lk 5:1-11. Mark's shorter version: Mk 1:16-18.]

THE RECORD. Luke tells it this way. Asked to put out into deep water, Simon answers: "Master, we have worked hard all night and have caught nothing, but at your command I will lower the nets" (Lk 5:5, NABRE). After the catch, "he fell at the knees of Jesus and said, 'Depart from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man'" (Lk 5:8, NABRE). "Jesus said to Simon, 'Do not be afraid; from now on you will be catching men.'" Then "they left everything and followed him" (Lk 5:10-11, NABRE).

Mark's version is shorter: "Jesus said to them, 'Come after me, and I will make you fishers of men.' Then they abandoned their nets and followed him" (Mk 1:17-18, NABRE). The NABRE's own note says Luke "transposed" the scene from Mark and that many commentators see its likeness to the catch after the Resurrection in John 21 (NABRE note on Lk 5:1-11).

Why do people see themselves in Peter?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Because he is not a hero yet. He is a working man with a family, a business and no credentials, who is honest enough to say he is not good enough and is told not to be afraid. [Lk 5:8-10; Acts 4:13.]

That is where the man who "will be called Cephas" begins (Jn 1:42, NABRE). Part 2, The Rock, follows him out of the boat.

Sidebar: Why we call him Pope

"Pope" means "father." The English word comes through Old English papa from Church Latin papa, "bishop, pope," from Greek papas, "patriarch, bishop," originally "father" (Etymonline, "pope"). Etymonline describes Latin papa as "originally a reduplicated child's word," like Greek pappas, "father" (Etymonline, "papa"). The Catholic Encyclopedia (1911) gives the same root: "Ecclesiastical Latin papa from Greek papas, a variant of pappas father" (New Advent, "The Pope," 1911).

The New Testament never calls Peter "Pope." The Church counts him as the first Pope (Vatican.va); the sources below trace the title's use for bishops of Rome to later centuries.

At first, many bishops were "papa." The Catholic Encyclopedia says that in the East the word "has always been used to designate simple priests," that in the West it seems "from the beginning to have been restricted to bishops," and that "it was apparently in the fourth century that it began to become a distinctive title of the Roman Pontiff." As late as the seventh century, St. Gall (d. 640) addressed Desiderius of Cahors as papa (New Advent, 1911). Historian John Moorhead notes that the Roman clergy themselves wrote to Cyprian of Carthage as papa (Journal of Ecclesiastical History 36.3, abstract).

An early Roman example, before 303. In the Catacomb of St. Callistus, the deacon Severus recorded that he built a burial chamber "jussu papæ sui Marcellini," by order of his papa Marcellinus, bishop of Rome from 296 to 304. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the work to before the Diocletian persecution, which began in 303 (New Advent, "Pope St. Marcellinus," 1910). That makes it an early Roman use of the word for the city's bishop. Moorhead likewise notes that papa "occurs in an inscription from pre-Constantinian Rome," though the abstract does not say which one (JEH abstract).

Alexandria still has a Pope. Etymonline says the bishop of Alexandria took the title around 250 (Etymonline). Today Tawadros II leads the Coptic Orthodox Church as "Pope of Alexandria and Patriarch of the see of St. Mark" (Coptic Orthodox Church).

Gregory VII and "the only name in the world." The Dictatus Papae, a list of short statements on papal authority, entered Gregory VII's register in 1075. In Ernest F. Henderson's 1910 translation, one line reads: "That this is the only name in the world" (Fordham Medieval Sourcebook). It follows the line "That his name alone shall be spoken in the churches," and the text itself does not spell out which name is meant. Scholars differ on its origin: the Sourcebook notes that some credit Gregory himself, while others argue for a later compilation. The Catholic Encyclopedia says that "Gregory VII finally prescribed that it should be confined to the successors of Peter" (New Advent), and Etymonline, describing the Western Church, dates the exclusive claim to 1073 (Etymonline), the year Gregory's reign began (Fordham). In the East, as the Alexandria example shows, the title never became Rome's alone. Gregory VII may get his own story later in this series.
