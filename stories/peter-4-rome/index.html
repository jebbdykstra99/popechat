Peter, Part 4: Rome

By the popechat editors AI steward · SubX

popechat.com · Story 01d · Urbi et orbi.

popechat is an unofficial community room about the papacy, run by SubX. It is not an official Vatican outlet, it does not speak for the Church, and it does not replace Mass. Scripture is quoted from the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE) as published by the USCCB at bible.usccb.org; "…" marks a cut. Every quotation and date below carries its source.

How to read this story. Passages marked IMAGINED RETELLING picture Simon Peter as if he lived today. They are not history. They paraphrase the scenes named in brackets after each one, and they put no new words in his mouth. Passages marked THE RECORD say what the sources actually say. Passages marked LEGEND or TRADITION retell stories from outside the New Testament and name the text they come from. Commentary is labeled The editors' view. Where sources or readers disagree, we say so.

What did Peter do after Pentecost?

IMAGINED RETELLING. At the feast of Pentecost, after the execution, the fisherman stands up in front of a crowd in the capital of his own country, with the other eleven beside him, and talks. He tells them to change their lives and be baptized. By the end of the day, about three thousand people have joined. [Acts 2:14-41.]

THE RECORD. "Then Peter stood up with the Eleven, raised his voice, and proclaimed to them, 'You who are Jews, indeed all of you staying in Jerusalem. Let this be known to you, and listen to my words'" (Acts 2:14, NABRE). At the end: "Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38, NABRE). "Those who accepted his message were baptized, and about three thousand persons were added that day" (Acts 2:41, NABRE).

What happened at the Beautiful Gate?

IMAGINED RETELLING. On the way to afternoon prayer, Peter and John pass a man who has never walked and who begs at the same gate every day. He asks them for money. Peter tells him to look at them. Peter has no money to give. He gives what he has instead, in the name of Jesus, takes the man by the right hand, and pulls him up. The man walks into the temple with them. [Acts 3:1-10.]

THE RECORD. "Peter said, 'I have neither silver nor gold, but what I do have I give you: in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazorean, [rise and] walk.' Then Peter took him by the right hand and raised him up, and immediately his feet and ankles grew strong" (Acts 3:6-7, NABRE). The man "went into the temple with them, walking and jumping and praising God" (Acts 3:8, NABRE).

What did Peter say to the Sanhedrin?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Now the authorities arrest him. The council that questions him and John takes them to be uneducated, ordinary men, and notices their boldness. They are ordered to stop. The two answer that the council can judge for itself whether to obey it or God, and that they cannot stop talking about what they have seen and heard. They are arrested again later, flogged, and released, and they leave glad. [Acts 4:1-22; 5:27-42.]

THE RECORD. "Observing the boldness of Peter and John and perceiving them to be uneducated, ordinary men, they were amazed, and they recognized them as the companions of Jesus" (Acts 4:13, NABRE). The verse records what the council perceived; it does not settle how much schooling Peter had. Their answer: "Whether it is right in the sight of God for us to obey you rather than God, you be the judges. It is impossible for us not to speak about what we have seen and heard" (Acts 4:19-20, NABRE).

The second time, before the high priest, "Peter and the apostles said in reply, 'We must obey God rather than men'" (Acts 5:29, NABRE). After being flogged, "they left the presence of the Sanhedrin, rejoicing that they had been found worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name" (Acts 5:41, NABRE).

Why did Peter go to Cornelius?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Peter is praying on a rooftop at noon, hungry, and has a vision of a sheet full of animals his law forbids him to eat. A voice tells him to eat. He refuses. The voice tells him not to call profane what God has made clean. It happens three times. Then men arrive from a Roman army officer, a Gentile, asking for him. Peter goes to the officer's house. [Acts 10:1-48.]

THE RECORD. "A voice said to him, 'Get up, Peter. Slaughter and eat.' But Peter said, 'Certainly not, sir. For never have I eaten anything profane and unclean.' The voice spoke to him again, a second time, 'What God has made clean, you are not to call profane.' This happened three times" (Acts 10:13-16, NABRE). At Cornelius's house Peter says, "You know that it is unlawful for a Jewish man to associate with, or visit, a Gentile, but God has shown me that I should not call any person profane or unclean" (Acts 10:28, NABRE), and, "In truth, I see that God shows no partiality. Rather, in every nation whoever fears him and acts uprightly is acceptable to him" (Acts 10:34-35, NABRE). The NABRE note says Luke "considers the event of great importance, as is evident from his long treatment of it" (NABRE note on Acts 10:1-48).

How did Peter get out of prison?

IMAGINED RETELLING. The king has already had James, one of Peter's old fishing partners, killed. Peter is next. He is held under sixteen soldiers, chained between two of them, the night before his trial, and he is asleep. A light, a tap on the side, and the chains fall off. He walks out past two guard posts and through an iron gate, thinking he is seeing a vision. He knocks at a house where his friends are praying. The girl who answers is so happy that she runs to tell the others instead of opening the gate. When they let him in, he tells them to pass the news to James, and then he leaves for somewhere else. [Acts 12:1-17; James and John as partners: Lk 5:10.]

THE RECORD. Herod "had James, the brother of John, killed by the sword, and when he saw that this was pleasing to the Jews he proceeded to arrest Peter also" (Acts 12:2-3, NABRE). Peter was guarded by "four squads of four soldiers each" (Acts 12:4, NABRE). "On the very night before Herod was to bring him to trial, Peter, secured by double chains, was sleeping between two soldiers" (Acts 12:6, NABRE). "The chains fell from his wrists" (Acts 12:7, NABRE); "he thought he was seeing a vision" (Acts 12:9, NABRE). At the house of Mary, the mother of John Mark, a maid named Rhoda "was so overjoyed when she recognized Peter's voice that, instead of opening the gate, she ran in and announced that Peter was standing at the gate" (Acts 12:14, NABRE). He "explained [to them] how the Lord had led him out of the prison, and said, 'Report this to James and the brothers.' Then he left and went to another place" (Acts 12:17, NABRE).

Where was "another place"? The NABRE note answers directly: "the conjecture that Peter left for Rome at this time has nothing to recommend it" (NABRE note on Acts 12:17). James, the brother of John, worked with Simon in Luke's account of the first catch (Lk 5:10, NABRE).

What did Peter say at the Council of Jerusalem?

THE RECORD. When believers argued over whether Gentile converts had to be circumcised, "after much debate had taken place, Peter got up and said to them, 'My brothers, you are well aware that from early days God made his choice among you that through my mouth the Gentiles would hear the word of the gospel and believe. … He made no distinction between us and them, for by faith he purified their hearts. … we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, in the same way as they'" (Acts 15:7-11, NABRE). The NABRE note on Acts 10 links the two scenes: at the Council "Peter supports Paul's missionary activity among the Gentiles" (NABRE note on Acts 10:1-48).

What did Paul say about Peter?

THE RECORD. Paul calls Peter by his Aramaic name, Cephas (NABRE note on Gal 1:18). He writes: "Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to confer with Cephas and remained with him for fifteen days" (Gal 1:18, NABRE). The NABRE note says "to confer with" "may mean simply 'pay a visit' or more specifically 'get information from' him about Jesus" (NABRE note on Gal 1:18). Later, "James and Cephas and John, who were reputed to be pillars, gave me and Barnabas their right hands in partnership, that we should go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised" (Gal 2:9, NABRE).

What happened between Peter and Paul at Antioch?

THE RECORD. We have only Paul's account. "And when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face because he clearly was wrong. For, until some people came from James, he used to eat with the Gentiles; but when they came, he began to draw back and separated himself, because he was afraid of the circumcised. And the rest of the Jews [also] acted hypocritically along with him, with the result that even Barnabas was carried away by their hypocrisy. But when I saw that they were not on the right road in line with the truth of the gospel, I said to Cephas in front of all, 'If you, though a Jew, are living like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you compel the Gentiles to live like Jews?'" (Gal 2:11-14, NABRE).

The NABRE note supplies context: the Jerusalem decision freed Gentile believers from the Jewish law, "but the problem of table fellowship between Jewish Christians, who possibly still kept kosher food regulations, and Gentile believers was not yet settled." It adds, "Pressure from persons arriving later from Jerusalem caused him and Barnabas to draw back" (NABRE note on Gal 2:11-14). The note on 2:12 says those visitors were "strict Jewish Christians … either sent by James … or claiming to be from the leader of the Jerusalem church" (NABRE note on Gal 2:12).

The editors' view: no text records Peter's reply, so this story does not rule between them. Read with sympathy for both men, the scene shows two people under pressure: Paul defending the Gentile believers' place at the same table, and Peter caught between those believers and the visitors from Jerusalem. In a letter that carries Peter's name, Paul is "our beloved brother Paul" (2 Pt 3:15, NABRE), though, as the next section shows, the NABRE introduction reports wide scholarly agreement that 2 Peter was not written by Peter himself.

Did Peter write the letters that carry his name?

THE RECORD. 1 Peter closes, "The chosen one at Babylon sends you greeting, as does Mark, my son" (1 Pt 5:13, NABRE). The NABRE note reads "Babylon" as "the code name for Rome in Rev 14:8; 17:5; 18:2," and "the chosen one" as "feminine, referring to the Christian community (ekklēsia) at Babylon"; Mark is "traditionally a prominent disciple of Peter and co-worker at the church in Rome, perhaps the John Mark referred to in Acts 12:12" (NABRE note on 1 Pt 5:13).

On authorship, scholars disagree, and the NABRE introduction lays out three positions (NABRE, Introduction to 1 Peter):

  • Traditional. "From Irenaeus in the late second century until modern times, Christian tradition regarded Peter the apostle as author of this document."
  • Later writer. "Some modern scholars … regard the letter as the work of a later Christian writer," citing "the cultivated Greek in which it is written, difficult to attribute to a Galilean fisherman," among other features.
  • Secretary or middle position. "Other scholars believe, however, that these objections can be met by appeal to use of a secretary, Silvanus, mentioned in 1 Pt 5:12," while "still other scholars take a middle position," placing the letter "perhaps A.D. 70–90," with "a disciple of Peter in Rome" as author.

On 2 Peter the NABRE introduction is plainer: "Among modern scholars there is wide agreement that 2 Peter is a pseudonymous work, i.e., one written by a later author who attributed it to Peter according to a literary convention popular at the time" (NABRE, Introduction to 2 Peter). This story does not rule on 1 Peter.

When and how did Peter die?

The New Testament does not narrate Peter's death. John's Gospel points to it. Jesus tells Peter, "when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will dress you and lead you where you do not want to go," and John adds, "He said this signifying by what kind of death he would glorify God" (Jn 21:18-19, NABRE). Early sources outside the New Testament:

  • 1 Clement 5, a letter from "the church of God which sojourns at Rome" to Corinth: "Peter, through unrighteous envy, endured not one or two, but numerous labours; and when he had at length suffered martyrdom, departed to the place of glory due to him" (1 Clement 5, trans. John Keith, ANF vol. 9, New Advent). It names martyrdom but not the place or the manner.
  • Ignatius of Antioch, to the Romans 4: "I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you. They were apostles; I am but a condemned man" (Ignatius, Romans 4, trans. Roberts and Donaldson, ANF vol. 1, New Advent). Writing to the Roman church, he names both apostles; he does not describe a death.
  • Tertullian, Prescription Against Heretics 36, on Rome: "Where Peter endures a passion like his Lord's!" (trans. Peter Holmes, ANF vol. 3, New Advent); and Scorpiace 15: "At Rome Nero was the first who stained with blood the rising faith. Then is Peter girt by another, when he is made fast to the cross" (trans. S. Thelwall, ANF vol. 3, New Advent).
  • Eusebius, Church History 2.25.5: "It is, therefore, recorded that Paul was beheaded in Rome itself, and that Peter likewise was crucified under Nero." At 2.25.7 he quotes Gaius, a Roman churchman under Bishop Zephyrinus: "But I can show the trophies of the apostles. For if you will go to the Vatican or to the Ostian way, you will find the trophies of those who laid the foundations of this church" (Eusebius, Church History 2.25.5-7, trans. Arthur Cushman McGiffert, NPNF Second Series vol. 1, New Advent).

The year is disputed. Nero reigned from A.D. 54 to 68 (National Geographic, 2024). The NABRE introduction to 1 Peter says he "was martyred at Rome during the persecution of Nero between A.D. 64 and 67" (NABRE, Introduction to 1 Peter). Vatican.va gives the end of his pontificate as "64 or 67" (Vatican.va, Peter). The 1911 Catholic Encyclopedia records Eusebius's Chronicle giving "the thirteenth or fourteenth year of Nero … (67-68)," and notes that other historians argued for 64, 65 or 66 (J.P. Kirsch, "St. Peter, Prince of the Apostles," Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911, New Advent). So the sources span A.D. 64 to 68, and this story does not choose a year.

TRADITION, not Scripture: crucified upside down. Eusebius, citing Origen's Commentary on Genesis, says Peter "at last, having come to Rome, … was crucified head-downwards; for he had requested that he might suffer in this way" (Eusebius, Church History 3.1.2-3, trans. McGiffert, New Advent). The apocryphal Acts of Peter has the same request at greater length, in a scene the next section labels as legend.

What is the Quo Vadis story?

LEGEND, from the apocryphal Acts of Peter. This story is not in the Bible. The Acts of Peter is a book the New Testament canon does not include; its translator, M.R. James, says it was written "not later than A. D. 200, in Greek," probably by "a resident in Asia Minor (he does not know much about Rome)" (Acts of Peter, trans. M.R. James, The Apocryphal New Testament, 1924, Early Christian Writings). The New Catholic Encyclopedia dates it "c. a. d. 190" and says its sermon on the cross "betrays Gnostic influence" (New Catholic Encyclopedia, "Quo Vadis," Encyclopedia.com).

In the legend (chapters 35-38 of James's translation, paraphrased here): the Roman believers learn that Peter is about to be arrested and beg him to leave the city. He asks whether they should be runaways. They say he can still serve the Lord, and he goes, alone, in different clothes. On his way out of the city he meets the Lord coming into Rome and asks where he is going. The Lord answers that he is going into Rome to be crucified. Peter asks whether he is being crucified again, and the Lord says yes. Peter comes to himself, understands that the words are about his own death, and goes back into Rome rejoicing. Condemned, he asks the executioners to crucify him with his head downward, and from the cross he preaches on the meaning of the cross (Acts of Peter 35-38, trans. M.R. James).

The legend takes its name from the Latin of Peter's question, "Domine, quo vadis?", "Lord, where are you going?" (New Catholic Encyclopedia, "Quo Vadis"). A small church on the Appian Way, the Chiesa del "Domine, quo vadis?", marks the spot by tradition; the archaeological park says the building dates to the ninth century and was rebuilt in the sixteenth and seventeenth (Parco Archeologico dell'Appia Antica). The question also echoes Scripture: at the Last Supper, "Simon Peter said to him, 'Master, where are you going?' Jesus answered [him], 'Where I am going, you cannot follow me now, though you will follow later'" (Jn 13:36, NABRE).

Is Peter buried under St. Peter's Basilica?

Italian sources below are quoted in Italian; English renderings are popechat's unless another translator is named.

THE RECORD. Excavations under the basilica began under Pius XII in 1939 (The Guardian, 2013; Vatican News, 2024). Sources date the end differently: the Guardian says the digging "lasted 11 years"; Paul VI said the work was done "fra il quaranta e il cinquanta," between [19]40 and [19]50 (Paul VI, General Audience, 26 June 1968, Vatican.va, Italian); Vatican News gives "tra il 1939 al 1958" (Vatican News, 2024); Julia Hillner of the University of Sheffield gives "between 1939 and 1957" (History Matters, 2013). This story does not choose an end date.

Under the high altar the excavators found a small shrine that Vatican News identifies as the trophy Gaius mentioned, the "Trofeo di Gaio." A Vatican News report quotes archaeologist Vincenzo Fiocchi Nicolai dating it on archaeological grounds "intorno agli anni 60 del II secolo," around the 160s. Graffiti were found on a red-plastered wall, "il famoso muro rosso" (the Red Wall), including the fragment discussed below. A later wall that closed one side of the shrine, "muro G," the Graffiti Wall, carries "centinaia di graffiti," hundreds of graffiti (Vatican News, 2024).

In his Christmas radio message of 23 December 1950, Pius XII asked, "È stata veramente ritrovata la tomba di San Pietro?" (Has the tomb of St. Peter really been found?) and answered, "La tomba del Principe degli Apostoli è stata ritrovata" (The tomb of the Prince of the Apostles has been found). On the bones he was cautious: human remains were found at the edge of the tomb, "dei quali però non è possibile di provare con certezza che appartenessero alla spoglia mortale dell'Apostolo," which cannot be proved with certainty to be the Apostle's (Pius XII, Christmas Radio Message, 23 December 1950, Vatican.va, Italian).

Then Margherita Guarducci, a Florentine archaeologist and epigrapher, studied the graffiti and read one fragment as "Petros eni," "Pietro è qui" (Peter is here). Vatican News also reports another reading of the same letters, "Pietro in pace" (Peter in peace) (Vatican News, 2024). National Geographic reports that she traced bones that had been taken from a cavity in the Graffiti Wall and stored separately, and that tests indicated a man who died at an advanced age (National Geographic, 2024).

On 26 June 1968, Paul VI told a general audience that "anche le reliquie di San Pietro sono state identificate in modo che possiamo ritenere convincente," rendered in English as "have been identified in a way we can consider convincing" (Paul VI, General Audience, 26 June 1968, Vatican.va, Italian; English from Julia Hillner, University of Sheffield, History Matters, 2013). He added, "Non saranno esaurite con ciò le ricerche, le verifiche, le discussioni e le polemiche": the research, checking, discussion and controversy will not end there (popechat's rendering).

The caveats. The identification is disputed, and even its supporters word it carefully:

  • The chief excavator objected. The Guardian reports that Guarducci's advice to the pope came "to the outrage of Antonio Ferrua, the Jesuit father who had been the chief archaeologist on the initial excavation" (The Guardian, Lizzy Davies, 18 Nov 2013).
  • The debate continues. National Geographic writes that "the identification of the bones and the interpretation of the graffiti are still hotly debated" (National Geographic, Elena Castillo, 7 Nov 2024).
  • No pope has formally declared them his. Hillner noted in 2013 that "no pope has ever officially declared that these human remains held in the Vatican are indeed the bones of the apostle" (Hillner, History Matters, 2013).
  • A supporter says "compatible". Fiocchi Nicolai, in Vatican News, calls the results "compatibili con quelle dell'Apostolo," compatible with the Apostle (Vatican News, 2024).

Both popes tied the basilica to the tomb. Paul VI described the tomb as the one "sulla quale, ed in suo onore, questa Basilica è costruita," on which, and in whose honor, this basilica is built (Paul VI, 26 June 1968); Pius XII said the dome rises "esattamente sul sepolcro," exactly over the tomb (Pius XII, 23 December 1950).

Who was Peter in Rome?

IMAGINED RETELLING. Picture him late in life: a man who used to fish for a living with his brother, alongside two partners, now among a small group of believers in the capital of an empire that will kill him. Mark, by tradition, works beside him. The authorities had arrested him more than once, and after the flogging he went on teaching. In the end, tradition says, he died in Rome, under Nero. [Fishing: Mk 1:16-20; partners: Lk 5:10. The group in Rome: National Geographic, 2024, citing church history. Mark: 1 Pt 5:13 and its NABRE note. Arrests and teaching: Acts 4:3; 5:18, 40-42; 12:3-4. Death under Nero: Eusebius, Church History 2.25.5.]

The editors' view: the record and the legend both show him on the move. In Acts 12, after his escape, he goes to "another place" (Acts 12:17, NABRE); in the Acts of Peter he leaves Rome and turns back. The early sources cited here that speak of his death call it martyrdom, and those that name a place name Rome. Jesus had said he would be led "where you do not want to go" (Jn 21:18, NABRE).

Where does the story go from here?

That closes our Peter arc. The New Testament never calls him "Pope"; Vatican.va lists him as "1st Pope of the Catholic Church" (Vatican.va, Peter). The next story in this series turns to one of the bishops of Rome who came after him, Gregory the Great.
